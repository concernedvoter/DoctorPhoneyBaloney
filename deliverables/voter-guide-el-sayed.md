# Abdul El-Sayed: Policies, Donors and Expected Impact

2026-10-20

**Names.** Full legal name: Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed (by court order of December 9, 1991, Boone County, Missouri; no later change found). Publicly known as Abdul El-Sayed. Former name: Abdulrahman Mohamed, his name in the court record until that date. The name on his birth record is not verified.

**Birth, name change and the Social Security question.** These questions go to how complete his biography is, not to whether he can serve. A U.S. birth makes a person a citizen, and no document found shows a foreign birth.

- **Name change.** A Boone County, Missouri court changed his name from Abdulrahman Mohamed to Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed on December 9, 1991 (case 1391CC044966). The petition was brought by a next friend, Mohamed El-Sayed, which means he was a minor. The court's notice ran December 20 and 27, 1991 and January 3, 1992. The filings do not say why the name was changed. Documented.
- **Birth.** His birth date is reported as October 31, 1984. His birthplace is not independently verified: Bridge Michigan says Rochester Hills, the University of Michigan says Bloomfield Hills and a Soros fellowship profile says Metro Detroit. Michigan birth records are confidential, so the name on his birth record is also unverified. Reported.
- **Social Security.** A June 23, 2026 Michigan Enjoyer article says his Social Security number was issued in Missouri in 1987. No record supporting that was reviewed, and a Social Security number is private. Even if accurate, it would show where an application was filed, not where he was born. Not established.
- **Open.** In the material reviewed here, the campaign has not said where he was born or why the name was changed. The court file is public on request; only he can release his birth record.

This guide covers Abdul El-Sayed, Democratic nominee for the open Michigan U.S. Senate seat (Gary Peters is retiring), facing Republican Mike Rogers on November 3, 2026. Every claim below is sourced to FEC filings, his own published writing, or named news outlets, and rated Documented, Reported, Disputed or Not supported. Where a projection is offered rather than a fact, it is labeled as analysis.

**Medicare for All and jobs.** El-Sayed supports Medicare for All. In one video he says it "does away with the private insurance market" and leaves the federal government as the single payer, even as he says no one needs to be banned from private insurance. The best-known estimate, from economist Robert Pollin of UMass Amherst, is that a single-payer system would eliminate roughly 1.8 to 2 million mostly administrative jobs, about 800,000 of them at insurers, over two to four years. These are claims processors, billers, coders and brokers, and the jobs pay a median of about $54,000 at insurers. Supporters say savings from fewer administrators help pay for coverage and that care jobs would grow, though those pay less. Critics, including an industry-funded group, point to the layoffs. The current Sanders bill (S. 1506, introduced April 29, 2025) has a temporary worker assistance section for people who run the insurance system and may lose jobs. It covers up to five years, sets aside at least 1 percent of the national health budget, and lists wage replacement, retirement benefits, job training and placement, preferential hiring and education benefits, with no dollar amounts or age rules in the text (Congress.gov). An earlier secondary-source description of full salary to age 65 for workers 60 and older does not appear in that text and should not be relied on. This is a projection, not a measured result. No Michigan projection was found, so these are headcounts for scale, not forecasts: insurance carriers and related activities, which include non-health insurers, employed about 87,000 people in Michigan in December 2025, up from about 69,600 in January 2015 (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, from federal labor data), and Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan reports 12,196 employees worldwide, with 220 laid off in June 2025 amid rising costs and a $1 billion loss in 2024 (BCBSM; Becker's Payer). Congress would have to pass any such bill. Sources: [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/elections/analysis-a-health-care-overhaul-could-kill-2-million-jobs-and-thats-ok/), [People's World](https://www.peoplesworld.org/article/looking-after-workers-who-will-lose-their-jobs-under-medicare-for-all/), [Partnership for America's Health Care Future](https://americashealthcarefuture.org/icymi-medicare-for-all-means-layoffs/).

**Medicare for All and the economy.** Estimates disagree, and the answer depends on provider payment rates, how much care people use, how it is financed and how fast the change happens. The Congressional Budget Office modeled five single-payer designs in 2020 and projected that total national health spending in 2030 would change by between a $0.7 trillion decrease and a $0.3 trillion increase, with lower provider payments and less insurer overhead pushing spending down and more use of care pushing it up. Federal health costs would rise by $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion that year because the government would pay for much of what employers and households pay now. CBO gave no employment estimate and said higher taxes used for financing would reduce incentives to work and invest, slowing growth ([CBO, December 2020](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/56898)). A Mercatus Center analysis argues CBO's figures are too low because they assume provider payment cuts and demand that goes unmet, and puts new federal costs in 2030 at about $4 trillion or more ([Mercatus, April 2021](https://mercatus.org/research/policy-briefs/understanding-cbos-medicare-all-cost-estimates)). Mercatus leans free-market. Its earlier 2018 study by the same author put new federal spending at $32.6 trillion over the 10 years from 2022 to 2031, after a phase-in, and said doubling federal income tax receipts would not cover it (reported by Fox Business). On the other side, Robert Pollin says the biggest source of savings would be the administrative layoffs described above, but the source checked gives no dollar total for his savings, so none is reported here. The Penn Wharton Budget Model found everyone insured and far fewer people skipping care under every financing option, with outcomes differing by whether premiums, payroll taxes or deficits pay for it ([Penn Wharton](https://budgetmodel.wharton.upenn.edu/estimates/2020/1/30/medicare-for-all-comparison-of-financing-options)); its growth figures were not readable and are not reported here. Insurer job losses are one piece: hospitals and doctors could lose revenue if payment rates fall, while administrative savings could shift spending toward care. These studies date from 2019 to 2021, none is specific to Michigan, Congress would have to pass any plan, and El-Sayed has said people could keep private insurance, which would change every estimate. Rating: Reported, projections and not measured results.

**Addendum: what the Muslim Brotherhood is, and the case made against it.** The Brotherhood is an Islamist movement founded in Egypt in 1928 that seeks the moral and religious reform of societies and, in the long run, a unified Islamic state under sharia. A British government review completed in 2014 described a secretive, hierarchical cell structure ([UK Government review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a8076bfe5274a2e8ab504ab/53163_Muslim_Brotherhood_Review_-_PRINT.pdf)).

**The case critics make.** (1) That same British review found the group's ideology contrary to British values and interests, said it prefers gradual, non-violent change but has been willing to use violence when that seemed ineffective, and said it has supported Hamas. It found no link between the Brotherhood and terrorism in or against the UK. (2) In January 2026 the U.S. Treasury designated the Egyptian and Jordanian branches Specially Designated Global Terrorists, citing material support to Hamas, and the State Department designated the Lebanese branch a Foreign Terrorist Organization after rocket attacks on Israel following October 7, 2023 ([Charity & Security Network](https://charityandsecurity.org/analysis/muslim-brotherhood-organizations-in-egypt-jordan-and-lebanon-hit-with-u-s-terrorism-designations/)). (3) Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states designated the group in 2014, and Egypt cracked down on it after the 2013 coup. (4) Some critics argue it acts as a conveyor belt that moves followers toward extremism even when it does not itself order violence ([Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/posts/2016/03/is-the-muslim-brotherhood-a-terrorist-organization-or-a-firewall-against-violent-extremism)). (5) The British review noted some Brotherhood-linked literature promotes the view that Muslims cannot live under a non-Islamic government.

**The case scholars and defenders make.** Carnegie Endowment scholar Marc Lynch argues the Brotherhood is not a terrorist organization, is distinct from jihadist groups such as al-Qaeda, publicly endorsed nonviolence and elections, and long competed with those groups for recruits. He also says that buffer has weakened since 2013 and that today's picture is more uncertain. Civil liberties critics of the 2026 designations say they were not backed by public evidence, reflect lobbying by foreign governments, and could burden Muslim American charities. The designations cover specific branches, not the whole movement, and the movement's branches in many countries are loosely tied.

**What this means for a voting decision.** The designations and the ideology above describe the group, not any American candidate. A link to a group is not membership in it. Voters can weigh the rated record of what the candidate has done, said and signed, shown above and in the link map, against the case in this addendum, and reach their own conclusion.

Through the pre-general reports (Oct. 14), his campaign committee shows $53.83 million raised this cycle, $51.10 million spent and $2.73 million on hand; Rogers's committees show $23.02 million, $19.37 million and $3.90 million (FEC, retrieved Oct. 26; cutoffs differ). In Oct. 1 to 14 he raised $8.66 million and spent $9.71 million, leaving $2.73 million on hand (FEC pre-general report); Rogers raised nearly $2.5 million (Roll Call). FEC filings show Fighting for Michigan PAC also took $350,000 from Medicare for All PAC, $300,000 from the UAW's V-CAP, $250,000 from Forward Blue and $100,000 from Rep. Rashida Tlaib's campaign. As of October 15, outside groups had spent about $42 million for him and about $95 million for Rogers (Bridge Michigan). He pledges to refuse super PAC money; the super PAC sums above are independent spending he cannot legally direct, and as of July he had not publicly disavowed American Priorities ([JTA](https://www.jta.org/2026/07/07/politics/abdul-el-sayed-has-refused-super-pac-funding-an-anti-aipac-pac-says-it-will-spend-for-him-anyway)). His own campaign reports show $920.23 in-kind from Fig and Olive Action PAC and $3,500 from Javed. Nothing here is evidence of a quid pro quo, and no FEC complaint against these groups was found.

## What to expect: analysis, not a prediction

This section connects his stated positions to his funders' interests. It is our analysis of where alignment exists, not proof of control over his votes; he has no Senate voting record yet, and a senator is one of 100 votes, bound by committee assignments and the 60-vote threshold for most legislation.

- **Israel policy:** His own book calls for ending military aid to Israel, Egypt and Saudi Arabia. The two largest Israel-focused outside funders in his race, the Institute for Middle East Understanding ($400,000 to Fighting for Michigan PAC) and American Priorities ($815,700 spent in the race), are both organized around changing U.S. policy toward Israel. He pledges to refuse super PAC money and the law bars coordination, but as of July he had not publicly disavowed this spending. The alignment is real and consistent; voters should expect him to be among the most pro-conditioning-or-ending-aid votes in the Senate if elected, which was already his position before this money arrived.
- **Domestic progressive priorities:** His gun, health care and policing positions predate his current donor base and match the national progressive wing generally, not any single funder's identifiable interest. Expect him to caucus with the Senate's progressive bloc on these issues.
- **CAIR and Brotherhood-linked associations:** No donor money has been shown to buy a specific legislative outcome here; the documented pattern is reputational and relational (his father-in-law's donations and leadership roles, his sister's family history, his own CAIR speaking engagements), not a transaction tied to a bill. Expect continued scrutiny and campaign pressure on this point rather than a traceable policy outcome.
- **Open question:** Who funds Fig and Olive Action Inc remains unverified. Until that is known, any claim about what interest it represents is Not supported. FEC records show the $370,000 came from the nonprofit, not its PAC. Because 501(c)(4) groups need not disclose donors, the answer may stay hidden unless regulators or the group release it.
- **Voter bias, measured:** A Michigan State University survey found 37% of likely 2026 general-election voters said they are less likely to support a Muslim candidate. CAIR-Michigan reported three days of consecutive death threats following an anti-Islam protest in Dearborn. Voters should weigh the Brotherhood-association claims above against the documented fact that religion itself is shaping some votes independent of any specific claim's truth.

## Scope of impact if elected

| Level | What a senator actually controls | Expected impact |
|---|---|---|
| Local (Wayne County, Metro Detroit) | No direct authority; symbolic representation only | First Muslim, first Arab-American Michigan senator; no change to local policing, schools or zoning |

| National | One of 100 votes; committee assignments; the Senate's 60-vote threshold for most bills | Michigan is one of a handful of seats (with Georgia, North Carolina, Maine) that determines whether Democrats can approach a majority in a chamber Republicans hold 53-47; losing it forecloses that path. His specific votes would push for an assault weapons ban, ending the 1033 program, and Medicare for All, none of which he can pass alone |
| Global | Senate votes on arms sales, aid packages and treaties | His documented position (end military aid to Israel, Egypt and Saudi Arabia) is left of most sitting Democratic senators; in a narrowly divided Senate his vote carries more weight on rare margin votes; his election would be read internationally as a marker of Muslim-American political representation, independent of his actual votes |

None of this amounts to unilateral power. A senator votes, co-sponsors, sits on committees and uses the office's visibility; a senator does not set foreign policy or domestic law alone.
