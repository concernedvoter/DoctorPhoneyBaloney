# Abdul El-Sayed: His Research, Its Assumptions and Possible Biases

Prepared 2026-10-24

## Bottom line

Of his roughly 70 PubMed-indexed items (2008 to 2021), the 20 that bear on race, national origin, politics or insurance show no demeaning language or hostility toward any racial or national group. The published claims that a 2011 paper was "eugenic" or warned Arab women against non-Arab partners are not supported by the paper's text. The record does show consistent value commitments that shape his questions and sometimes go further than his data.

- **Social explanations first.** Race is treated as mainly social. Gaps are attributed to discrimination, poverty or "systemic racism," at times beyond what a study measured. Documented.
- **Policy advocacy inside research papers.** Between them, two coauthored 2021 papers recommend universal health care, living wages, paid sick leave and "disinvestment from carceral systems." Neither study tested those policies. Documented.
- **Culture as an explanation, in both directions.** Early birth studies suggested Arab culture and immigrant status protect newborns. A 2011 paper suggested Arab traditions may raise chronic-disease risk. A 2019 paper found the "immigrant health paradox" does not hold for Arab Americans in California. Documented.
- **Evidence against motivated reasoning.** He published null results that cut against convenient hypotheses, including no change in Arab American birth outcomes in Michigan after 9/11. Documented.

The clearest tie to Medicare for All is a 2021 paper he helped draft that calls universal health care "essential" to addressing racial inequities. None of his studies compared payment systems.

**What he favors.** Arab American visibility, immigrants, government and prevention, the poor and primary care. He published findings that cut against each. **Misogyny.** None in his research. His harsh remarks about female rivals are Documented; whether they were sexist is Disputed.

## How this was checked

- **Whose papers.** A PubMed search for "Abdulrahman El-Sayed" returns 72 items. Four belong to other researchers (a laboratory at the Polish Academy of Sciences and a team in Brussels). The other 68, plus two found under "El-Sayed AM," were kept because his listed affiliation, coauthors or email match: University of Michigan, Oxford, Columbia, the Detroit Health Department or Wayne State. Unrelated authors with the same initials in Egypt and Qatar were excluded, including Egyptian veterinary researchers named El-Sayed whose studies of goats and other livestock turn up in name searches. None of his 70 papers concerns animals.
- **What was read.** Full text where PubMed Central has it; otherwise the abstract. Quotes below were checked against the paper or the journal page.
- **What counts as bias.** Four tests: language that demeans or ranks a group; conclusions or policy claims that go beyond the study design; explanations assumed rather than measured; and whether results that cut against his views were published anyway. Holding a value, such as that health care is a right, is not by itself bias. It is noted where it shapes the work.
- **His role.** Author position is given for each paper. Where a contributor statement exists, it is used, since a fourth author on a six-person paper did not necessarily write its conclusions.
- **Limits.** His books, podcast and campaign statements are covered only where they connect to the research. The 2017 commentary and two early papers were read through abstracts and secondary quotes, as marked.

## Race and ethnicity in his research

He states his view of race plainly: it is mostly a stand-in for social conditions. The weak points come when a remaining gap is credited to racism that the study did not measure.

- **How he defines race (2014, sole author).** He calls race "a poorly defined and nonspecific term" and a proxy for other factors. He notes that genetic variation is greater within racial groups than between them, yet still calls genetic explanations "a viable hypothesis." He urges researchers to model the underlying exposures, such as income or discrimination, rather than race itself. Documented. Assessment: balanced; it does not rule out biology.
- **Genes versus environment in infant deaths (2015, first author).** Using 1.4 million Michigan births to Black, white and mixed-race married couples, the paper concludes that "environmental factors likely play the greater role." The trends it relied on were not statistically significant: the text says "neither change was significant," and none of its three interaction measures differed from the null. Documented. Assessment: the conclusion leans past the data, in the direction of his stated view.
- **COVID-19 deaths in Michigan (2021, third of ten; contributor statement says he helped draft).** The descriptive findings are strong. The interpretation says the disparities "result from systemic racism," which the study did not measure, and it had no individual income data. The discussion adds: "The anti-Black systemic racism in our political system is paralleled by the racialized nature of COVID-19." Documented. Assessment: an explanation taken from earlier literature and stated as a result.
- **Medical-school debt (2013, second of six).** Racial gaps in expected debt "roughly correlated with racial and ethnic disparities in income," so the paper names access to money, not discrimination, as "the central driver." Documented. Assessment: an example of not defaulting to racism as the cause.
- **Language.** Groups are compared in standard categories (Black, white, Arab, Hispanic, Asian). No slurs, ranking of groups, or demeaning descriptions were found in the papers read. Documented.

Overall: a consistent preference for social explanations, mainstream in his field, that sometimes outruns the evidence. Racial animus: Not supported.

## National origin, immigration and Arab American identity

This is where his work touches relationships across ethnic lines and assimilation after immigrating. The record shows an early hypothesis that immigrant culture protects health, later findings that cut the other way, and one paper whose wording invites criticism but does not say what critics claim.

**Interethnic couples (2011, first of two authors, with Sandro Galea)**

- **What it studied.** 21,621 births to Arab American mothers in Michigan, 2000 to 2005, with 594,431 births to white mothers as a comparison.
- **What it found.** Among Arab mothers, a non-Arab father was associated with 18% higher odds of preterm birth and 24% higher odds of late preterm birth. No link appeared among white mothers.
- **What it says.** "Interethnic mating may be associated with increased risk for adverse birth outcomes." It offers two possible explanations, both social: stress and discrimination faced by mixed couples, and differences in cultural practice. Of Arab mothers with non-Arab partners it writes, "On the whole, they broke away from cultural norms," meaning they were less often married, more educated, more often U.S.-born and more likely to smoke. It mentions genetics only as the explanation earlier studies gave for Black-white couples, and leans away from it.
- **What it recommends.** Only more research, including on discrimination against mixed couples and on Hispanic couples. It gives no advice about whom anyone should marry or have children with.
- **Wording.** "Mating" is the term used in the earlier studies it cites on Black-white, Chinese American and Japanese American couples. His CV reportedly lists the paper as "interethnic parentage"; the journal title says "mating."
- **Limits it admits.** Maternal education was the only income-type control, and residual confounding is possible.
- **Assessment.** Documented: the paper reports an association and treats departure from cultural norms as a possible risk, an idea it does not test and phrases in value-laden terms. Not supported: that it is "eugenic" or a warning to Arab women.

**Assimilation and acculturation**

- **2008, births after 9/11.** No rise in poor birth outcomes among Arab American mothers in Michigan after the attacks, unlike an earlier California study. A null result on discrimination, published anyway. Documented.
- **2009, Dearborn.** Arab mothers living in Dearborn had lower low-birth-weight risk than Arab mothers elsewhere in Michigan; white mothers in Dearborn did not. The authors read this as lower acculturation protecting newborns, but write that "future work should directly measure acculturation." Documented. The Daily Caller quotes the paper as saying minorities who "interact with the white majority" acculturate; not checked against the full text. Reported.
- **2009, preterm birth.** Arab mothers' lower preterm-birth risk disappeared after accounting for foreign birth, marriage and lower smoking. Documented.
- **2011, mortality.** Arab Americans in Michigan lived 1.4 to 2.0 fewer years than whites despite more education and income. The paper suggests "the paucity of a culture of explicit exercise, as well as a more corpulent image of beauty" in Arab tradition may raise chronic-disease risk, and cites a study tying acculturation to lower diabetes risk. It also says "pressure to acculturate out of fear of discrimination" may harm health. Documented.
- **2014, Mexican paradox.** The Mexican infant-mortality advantage faded over time, which the authors tie to "changing sociocultural norms" and changes in who immigrates. Documented.
- **2018 and 2019.** The updated review links acculturation to more exercise, less tobacco and more drinking. The 2019 California study concludes the immigrant health paradox "does not appear to apply to Arab Americans." Documented.
- **Assessment.** The early birth papers follow a mainstream idea in immigrant-health research: new immigrants often start healthier and lose some of that edge as they assimilate. His later work found mixed or no effects, and one paper faulted Arab culture, not American culture. "Assimilating to America is a health hazard" is Not supported as a summary of his work. A fair criticism: "culture" and "acculturation" were inferred from where mothers lived or were born, not measured.

**Arab American identity in data.** The 2017 commentary and later papers argue for an Arab or Middle Eastern identifier on health surveys and the Census, since Arab Americans are counted as white. This is open advocacy for data collection. Reported (full commentary not retrieved; read through a Boston University summary and the later papers).

**Israel, Palestine and foreign policy.** No paper in the verified record studies Israel, Palestine or U.S. Middle East policy. The 2009 and 2018 reviews mention the Arab-Israeli conflict and Middle East wars only as reasons Arab Americans face scrutiny. His public statements on Israel are covered in the main dossier.

## Political framing

His political commitments are open, progressive and consistent. They show most in his editorials and in the discussion sections of coauthored papers from 2016 to 2021, after he entered public office and politics.

- **Markets (2012 op-ed).** "Five Reasons Free Markets Don't Work in Health Care" argues that uncertainty, unequal information and short-term thinking defeat markets in health care, and that the U.S. leads medical innovation "because our government pumps tens of billions of dollars into health research." Documented (HuffPost; the page address carries his name, though the archived page shows no byline).
- **Government (2016 editorial, sole author).** Uses the Flint water crisis, "negligence under state emergency control," to argue that prevention is a public good, and states that "economic theory argues that government is the most efficient provider of public goods." It criticizes state budget cuts and agency restructuring without naming a party or the governor. Documented. Assessment: a contested economic premise stated as settled; the piece says it reflects his own opinion.
- **Physicians' politics (2015, second of five).** A survey of 2,355 medical students found liberals far more willing than conservatives to accept pay limits to widen access. The paper describes the divide as "a commitment to equity and social welfare among liberals and a commitment to free market ideals among conservatives." Documented. Assessment: an even-handed description of both sides; a response rate of about 2% limits it.
- **Incarceration (2021, fourth of six; advised on analysis).** The introduction states that jail incarceration "operates as an institutional product of structural racism." The interpretation calls for "disinvestment from carceral systems and investment in social and public health services." The study adjusted for county crime, poverty and unemployment, and concedes the link might be "a shared consequence of concentrated and racialised disadvantage." Documented. Assessment: a sound method for an association study; the policy call is not something it tested.
- **COVID-19 (2021, third of ten; helped draft).** The discussion says "Universal healthcare, living wages for all workers, and paid sick leave are essential public health interventions for addressing racial inequities," calls to "fundamentally restructure prison systems," and says the U.S. response shows "our society's continued negligence toward the wellbeing of all Americans." Its competing-interests statement reads "All authors have nothing to disclose." Documented. Assessment: these are policy positions, not findings of the study.

Overall: his politics appear as framing and recommendations. No evidence was found that data were altered or results withheld. Documented.

## Gender and women

No misogyny appears in his research. The charge rests on remarks about female political rivals, which a fact-checker found were aimed at policy or which he walked back.

**In his research**

- **Sex as a variable, reported both ways.** Men had twice the odds of complications after neurosurgery ([2012](https://doi.org/10.1227/NEU.0b013e31823e9706)), and male takotsubo patients died in hospital at more than twice the rate of women ([2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ahj.2012.04.010)). Women in the 2015 student survey were more willing than men to accept pay limits to widen access. Documented.
- **Attention to women's access to care.** Insurance helped the poorest women most with chronic-disease treatment and with avoiding borrowing for care (2015). His 2018 measure of failed insurance counts births outside a skilled facility. Documented.
- **Language.** No demeaning descriptions of women were found. The 2011 and 2015 birth papers note that a birth record can name a man who is not the biological father, a standard data caveat that the 2015 paper calls uncommon. Documented.

**In his remarks**

- **Haley Stevens, June 2026.** He called his primary rival "a suit with a large AIPAC bank account" and said someone should teach her to string coherent sentences together (Semafor). On July 17 he asked supporters to stop mocking her, and on CNN he said, "I don't think it's kind to make fun of somebody for how they talk." Documented.
- **Gretchen Whitmer, 2018, and Michelle Obama, 2010.** He called Whitmer "bought and sold" by corporate interests during the governor's race, and called the first lady's anti-obesity initiative "uninspired" in a Guardian column. Documented (as reported by Bridge Michigan).
- **The charge.** Rep. Hillary Scholten (D-Mich.), a Stevens supporter, called his remarks "deeply sexist attacks" on CNN in July 2026 ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/media/michigan-democrat-accuses-abdul-el-sayed-deeply-sexist-attacks-female-political-rivals)). Reported.
- **The fact check.** [Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-government/fact-checks-abdul-el-sayed-record-on-misogyny-defund-the-police-kamala-harris/) (July 29, 2026) found that ads accusing him of "disrespecting women" leave out "crucial context," and that the Whitmer and Obama criticisms appear rooted in policy, not gender. The ads were paid for by the United Democracy Project, an AIPAC-affiliated PAC. A 2022 complaint in a 2019 suit against Mount Sinai alleges he did not want anyone over 40 on a team; he is not a defendant, and it does not allege sex discrimination by him. Documented.

**Assessment.** The remarks about Stevens were personal and harsh, and he walked them back. Whether they reflect sexism is Disputed. A pattern of misogyny in his research or workplace record is Not supported.

## From research to Medicare for All

His own data support a narrow point: insurance matters, and coverage on paper is not the same as care. They never compare single-payer with other systems, and one study cuts the other way.

- **What his insurance studies show.** Across 48 lower- and middle-income countries, insurance was linked to more treatment for chronic disease and less borrowing to pay for care ([2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthpol.2015.07.006)). Across 42 such countries, 13% of insured people had insurance that failed them, so "attention should be paid to effectiveness" (2018). Income inequality predicted failed insurance ([2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czw156)). Documented.
- **What they do not show.** None compares public with private financing, or single-payer with multi-payer systems, in the United States. The country studies use survey data from 2002 to 2004. Documented.
- **A finding that cuts both ways.** In his 2011 neurosurgery study, Medicare and Medicaid patients had two to three times the odds of complications of privately insured patients. He read this as a socioeconomic gap; the data cannot separate the insurer from the patients' circumstances. Documented.
- **A finding that bears on cost.** Savings estimates for Medicare for All depend on paying doctors and hospitals less. His 2015 survey found medical students slightly less willing than practicing physicians to accept pay limits (about 62% against 67%), and students with large debts less willing still. Documented. Assessment: his own work points to the pressure on which his policy depends, without resolving it.
- **Where research and platform meet.** The only research paper found that recommends universal health care is the 2021 COVID-19 paper, as a policy statement. His cost claims as a candidate, including his October 20, 2026 statement that Medicare for All would be "25% cheaper," rest on assumptions his research did not test. Independent estimates run from modest national savings under low payment rates (CBO, 2020; Mercatus, 2018) to higher national spending (Urban Institute, 2019; RAND, 2019), with a large rise in federal spending in every case. Reported.

The wider evidence on the policy is in the main dossier and the voter guides.

## Strongest critiques and strongest defenses

**Critiques that hold up**

- **Explanations assumed, not measured.** "Culture" and "acculturation" were inferred from where mothers lived or were born. "Systemic racism" was credited for gaps the 2021 COVID-19 study did not explain.
- **Conclusions past the data.** The 2015 infant-mortality paper favors environment on non-significant trends. The 2021 jail and COVID-19 papers recommend policies they did not test.
- **Cultural generalizations, in both directions.** "They broke away from cultural norms" (2011, of Arab mothers with non-Arab partners); "the paucity of a culture of explicit exercise, as well as a more corpulent image of beauty" (2011, of Arab tradition).
- **Loaded wording in research text.** "The prison industrial complex" and "our society's continued negligence" (2021 COVID-19 paper).
- **One school of thought.** Much of the work is with one mentor, Sandro Galea, in social epidemiology, where structural explanations are the norm. Behavior and family structure (marriage, smoking) appear in the birth studies, but market-based explanations are never the hypothesis tested.

**Defenses that hold up**

- **Inconvenient results published.** No rise in poor Arab American birth outcomes after 9/11; in a simulation, network-targeted obesity programs did no better than random ones (2013); the immigrant health paradox did not hold in California (2019); racial debt gaps tracked income (2013); and Arab Americans' shorter lives were partly blamed on Arab tradition (2011).
- **Limits stated openly.** The papers name their thin income controls, possible confounding and data-quality problems.
- **Mainstream methods and journals.** Large state registries, fixed-effects models and peer review in Pediatrics, AJPH, Lancet Public Health and others. No retractions or corrections were found, though the check was not exhaustive.
- **Mainstream questions.** Studies of birth outcomes among mixed-ethnicity couples, and of the immigrant health paradox, were established lines of research before he joined them.
- **An outside view.** Sally Satel of the American Enterprise Institute, asked by a critical outlet: "These papers look sound to me."
